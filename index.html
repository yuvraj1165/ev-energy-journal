The short version

It wasn't always cheap. Timing made the difference.

I got my first EV in April and wanted to know what it would really cost to run. I paired the Geely EX5 with an Andersen A3 home charger and Octopus Agile, where electricity prices change every half-hour.

The idea is simple: let charging follow the cheaper slots where possible, rather than automatically filling the battery at any price. Over six months that came to about 1,018 kWh of home charging for a net energy cost of £67.23.

That average hides some big swings. May was the reality check; September was the surprise.

One month the grid effectively paid me to charge the car — the average price went negative.

When I bought my Geely EX5, I expected home charging to be cheaper than running a petrol or diesel car. What I didn't expect was that, occasionally, electricity would effectively pay me to charge it.

I didn't manage this by constantly watching prices or rearranging my life around the cheapest half-hour. The car simply charged whenever the price met the conditions I'd set — and over six months, that was enough to land 42.4% of its energy in negative-price slots.

So, how does this actually work?

Octopus Agile changes its electricity price every 30 minutes, giving 48 different half-hourly prices across the day, and the next day's prices are published each afternoon at around 4pm. The Andersen can then use a price ceiling to decide when charging should take place — it doesn't need me sitting there monitoring the market. If the price falls below the threshold, it charges. If the price is above it, it waits.

And if the price goes negative? That's when things get particularly interesting. During a negative-price period, the electricity unit price is below £0/kWh — in simple terms, the wholesale market is effectively paying for consumption during that period. For an EV, which can shift a large chunk of its electricity use to another time, that's a very useful opportunity.

A public rapid charger costs me around 60p/kWh or more, so almost any home price is a win — but on Agile I'm not really chasing "cheap," I'm chasing free or negative.

How I think about the price tiers

60p+/kWh — a public rapid charger. This is the cost I'm trying to avoid.

Around 7p/kWh or below — genuinely cheap, but I only take it if the car needs charge now and I can't wait for a better window. It's my "can't-wait" fallback, not the plan.

0p/kWh or below — the sweet spot. I plan charging into these windows, and sometimes the price is negative, so I'm actually paid to fill the battery.

So the question isn't "is 5p cheap?" — of course it is. It's "why pay 5p when, a bit of patience later, the same energy is free or negative?" The 7p line is just my limit for when patience isn't an option.

But what about the rest of the house?

This was one of the questions I most wanted to answer. If EV charging is so cheap, is the saving simply being offset by expensive electricity elsewhere in the house?

Not really. Over the same period, from 13 April through the end of September, my household — excluding the EV charging — used 825.5 kWh, costing £171.11, an average of 20.7p/kWh. There was also the fixed standing charge of 66.26p per day, which came to £113.27 over that period. So the total household electricity cost, including the standing charge, was £284.38 — compare that with £67.23 for EV charging.

The important difference isn't that the car's electricity is somehow different from the house's. It's that the EV gives me something most household use doesn't: flexibility. Most consumption happens when it happens — the fridge doesn't care what the Agile price is, and neither do the lights. Cooking, computers and televisions all have to run when I need them.

The EV is different. If I need 20 or 30 kWh in the battery, I generally don't care whether it goes in tonight, tomorrow morning or tomorrow afternoon — only that the car's charged when I need to drive it. That flexibility is what lets the charging load slide towards the cheapest periods, and it's where the saving comes from.

When did the negative hours land?

Negative pricing isn't random. Over the six months it has fallen overwhelmingly on weekends and in the middle of the day — when demand is low and there's plenty of wind and solar on the grid. That is also, inconveniently, when the car is most likely to be out.

What I've learned

Flexibility is the real advantage

Let the charger do the waiting

A battery doesn't mind when it fills, only that it's full when I need it. A price ceiling hands that decision to the market instead of a clock, and avoids most expensive slots without me watching prices all day.

Routine matters

If the car is away during a cheap window, that opportunity is missed — and it often is. On weekends 59% of negative half-hours went unused, and on my office days 88% did. Convenience and real driving needs still come first.

One season isn't a forecast

Six months show what's possible, not what every driver will pay — May proved Agile isn't magically cheap. A full year, across all the seasons, will give a fairer picture.

For the detail-minded

Method & caveats

Is the £67.23 an estimate?

No. That £67.23 covers 1,018 kWh of home charging across 6 months, and it isn't based on an assumed rate — it's built from the actual half-hourly Agile prices tied to the electricity my charger used. That distinction matters on Agile, because multiplying total kWh by a single average rate can give a very different, misleading answer. Monthly figures are each rounded before display, so they won't always sum exactly to the six-month total; the total itself is calculated from the unrounded underlying data.

Does it include standing charges or public charging?

No. It's the net unit-energy cost of home EV charging only. Household standing charges, public rapid charging, insurance, servicing, tyres and depreciation are all excluded. The household electricity comparison above is a separate figure with its own scope.

On longer trips I do use public rapid chargers, and those sessions aren't in this total — they'd lift the real per-mile cost of running the car. What this measures is specifically what home charging on Agile costs, which is where the car gets the overwhelming majority of its energy.

How reliable are the figures, and how were the missed slots counted?

EV energy is metered directly by the home charger, with every half-hour priced at the real Octopus Agile rate, grouped by calendar month. April runs from the 13th only, since that's when this account joined Agile. Figures are aggregated and rounded from the underlying half-hourly data and cross-checked against Octopus billing statements for April–September 2026.

On the missed negative slots: 37% of missed office-day slots and 66% of missed weekend slots followed a large charge in the prior day or two, suggesting the battery was often already topped up rather than the car simply being away. Of the ten biggest of those prior top-ups, only four were at positive rates, averaging just 3.1p — the rest were themselves at negative or near-zero prices.
