When I bought my Geely EX5, I expected home charging to be considerably cheaper than running a petrol or diesel car.

What I didn't expect was that, occasionally, electricity would effectively pay me to charge it.

I've now been tracking my home charging from April through September 2026, using Octopus Agile with my Andersen A3 7 kW home charger. Over that period, the car used roughly 1,018 kWh for around 4,000 miles of driving. The total charging cost? Just £67.23 — an effective cost of around 1.7p per mile.

And perhaps the most surprising number of all: 42.4% of the electricity used to charge the car was consumed during periods when the Agile price was negative.

I didn't do this by constantly watching electricity prices or rearranging my life around the cheapest half-hour. The car simply charged whenever the price met the conditions I'd set.

So, how does this actually work?

My setup is fairly simple:

  • Car: Geely EX5
  • Charger: Andersen A3, 7 kW
  • Tariff: Octopus Agile
  • Charging method: price-based scheduling
  • Prices: every afternoon at around 4pm, the next day's prices are published

Octopus Agile changes its electricity price every 30 minutes, giving 48 different half-hourly prices across the day. The Andersen can then use a price ceiling to decide when charging should take place — it doesn't need me sitting there monitoring the market. If the price falls below the threshold, it charges. If the price is above it, it waits.

And if the price goes negative? That's when things get particularly interesting. During a negative-price period, the electricity unit price is below £0/kWh — in simple terms, the wholesale market is effectively paying for consumption during that period. For an EV, which can shift a large chunk of its electricity use to another time, that's a very useful opportunity.

A public rapid charger costs me around 60p/kWh or more, so almost any home price is a win — but on Agile I'm not really chasing "cheap," I'm chasing free or negative.

How I think about the price tiers

60p+/kWh — a public rapid charger. This is the cost I'm trying to avoid.

Around 7p/kWh or below — genuinely cheap, but I only take it if the car needs charge now and I can't wait for a better window. It's my "can't-wait" fallback, not the plan.

0p/kWh or below — the sweet spot. I plan charging into these windows, and sometimes the price is negative, so I'm actually paid to fill the battery.

So the question isn't "is 5p cheap?" — of course it is. It's "why pay 5p when, a bit of patience later, the same energy is free or negative?" The 7p line is just my limit for when patience isn't an option.

September — the month the grid paid me to charge

This is the one I keep checking twice. I charged 207.2 kWh, and the average price came out at −1.5p/kWh — not "nearly free," actually negative, across the whole month. 86% of the charging fell in negative-price periods, and the net result was a credit: the charging cost came to −£3.15. The value of those negative windows more than offset everything I paid in the positive ones. One session on the 20th alone pulled 57.6 kWh over eight and a half hours, almost entirely at negative prices. But what about the rest of the house?

This was one of the questions I most wanted to answer. If EV charging is so cheap, is the saving simply being offset by expensive electricity elsewhere in the house?

Not really. Over the same period, from 13 April through the end of September, my household — excluding the EV charging — used 825.5 kWh, costing £171.11, an average of 20.7p/kWh. There was also the fixed standing charge of 66.26p per day, which came to £113.27 over that period. So the total household electricity cost, including the standing charge, was £284.38 — compare that with £67.23 for EV charging.

The important difference isn't that the car's electricity is somehow different from the house's. It's that the EV gives me something most household use doesn't: flexibility. Most consumption happens when it happens — the fridge doesn't care what the Agile price is, and neither do the lights. Cooking, computers and televisions all have to run when I need them.

The EV is different. If I need 20 or 30 kWh in the battery, I generally don't care whether it goes in tonight, tomorrow morning or tomorrow afternoon — only that the car's charged when I need to drive it. That flexibility is what lets the charging load slide towards the cheapest periods, and it's where the saving comes from.

I wasn't even optimising aggressively

Worth being clear about this: I wasn't turning my life upside down to chase negative prices. My routine stayed broadly the same. Most of the time I just let the Andersen A3 follow the price ceiling I'd set; occasionally I'd spot a particularly cheap window and shift my plans, and sometimes I had to charge at a higher price because the car was needed for a trip. That's ordinary EV ownership.

And there were plenty of cheap and negative-price periods I simply couldn't use, because the car wasn't at home. Looking back at the data, 59% of negative half-hours on weekends went unused, while 88% of negative half-hours on office days went unused. The reason is obvious enough: the car isn't parked on the driveway all day, every day.

Tuesdays and Thursdays are my office days, Saturdays in summer are usually cricket, and Sundays tend to be outings here and there — which is most of why these slots get missed. Most of those misses turn out to be fairly low-stakes, though: when I check what I'd charged just before, it was usually already at a cheap or negative price itself, so missing the next window typically wasn't much of a loss. (The full breakdown is in the methodology note at the end, for anyone curious.)

So these results aren't a theoretical maximum. They're what happened while using the car completely normally.

When did the negative hours land?

Negative pricing isn't random. Over the six months it has fallen overwhelmingly on weekends and in the middle of the day — when demand is low and there's plenty of wind and solar on the grid. That is also, inconveniently, when the car is most likely to be out.

What I've learned so far

After six months, my biggest takeaway isn't that Octopus Agile is always cheap. It isn't — May proved that.

The real lesson is about timing. A battery doesn't mind when it fills, only that it's full when I need it — so I can hand that decision to the market instead of a clock. Some half-hours cost a few pence, some cost a more ordinary rate, and some pay me to take the energy. Averaged over six months, that willingness to wait is the whole trick: the car behaves less like an appliance I switch on and more like a flexible load the grid can lean on when it has power to spare.

Is this the maximum saving I could achieve?

Definitely not. I still missed plenty of cheap and negative-price opportunities because the car wasn't at home. I could theoretically be more deliberate about when I charge, particularly at weekends and on days when I'm working from home.

But that's not really the point of this experiment. I don't want to spend my life checking electricity prices — the whole attraction of automated charging is precisely that I shouldn't have to. I'd rather let the charger do the work and see what the numbers look like afterwards.

I'll keep tracking it

Six months is interesting. But a full year will be much more useful. The seasons will change. My driving will change. Electricity prices will change. And there will inevitably be months that look completely different from April, June or July.

One honest caveat before anyone reads too much into that £67: these figures are home charging only. On longer trips I do use public rapid chargers; how I keep that cost down is a story for another post. Those sessions aren't in this total, and they'd lift the real per-mile cost of running the car. What this experiment measures is specifically what home charging on Agile costs, which is where the car gets the overwhelming majority of its energy.

So I'm going to keep adding the numbers as they come in. The question I'm interested in now is simple: what will a full year of real-world EV home charging actually cost me? I'll find out.

Methodology & extra detail, for the data-curious

Household and EV figures are grouped by calendar month rather than Octopus billing period, and April specifically runs from the 13th only, since that's when this account joined Agile — no EV charging or Agile pricing applies before that date. Figures are aggregated and rounded from the underlying half-hourly data, and have been cross-checked against Octopus billing statements for April–September 2026.

On the missed negative slots: 37% of missed office-day slots and 66% of missed weekend slots followed a large charge in the prior day or two, suggesting the battery was often already topped up rather than the car simply being away. Of the ten biggest of those prior top-ups, only four were at positive rates, averaging just 3.1p — the rest were themselves at negative or near-zero prices. And purely as a hypothetical ceiling, not something I actually did: a second car filling every one of the 183 missed slots at my charger's usual rate could have taken on roughly 620 kWh, for a net credit of around £20.
